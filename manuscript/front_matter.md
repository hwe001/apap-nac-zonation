# When does N-acetylcysteine stop working? Treatment timing, ingestion pattern, and hepatic zonation in a model of acetaminophen hepatotoxicity

## Study Highlights

**What is the current state of knowledge?**

- N-acetylcysteine (NAC), the standard antidote for acetaminophen poisoning,
  loses efficacy when started more than ~8 h after ingestion; this 8-hour rule
  is empirical, derived from clinical cohorts rather than mechanism.
- Zonation-resolved models of acetaminophen hepatotoxicity predict injury but
  contain no treatment term, and all impose literature-derived enzyme
  gradients rather than measured ones.

**What does this study add?**

- A PK-calibrated NAC term and a staggered-ingestion term embedded in a
  zonation-resolved model, reproducing the clinical 8-hour rule from first
  principles.
- Measured human enzyme gradients widen the predicted antidote window
  substantially relative to the assumed gradients used by prior models — the
  zonation assumption propagates into treatment predictions, not only injury
  predictions.
- At fixed total dose, staggered ingestion is metabolically neutral: the
  clinical danger of staggered overdose reflects late presentation, not
  pattern-specific metabolism.

## Abstract

N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is the standard antidote in acetaminophen poisoning,
but its efficacy declines sharply when treatment starts more than ~8 h after
ingestion — an empirical observation without a mechanistic model behind it.
Zonation-resolved models of acetaminophen hepatotoxicity predict injury but
have never represented treatment, and all impose literature-derived enzyme
gradients. Here we embed a PK-calibrated NAC term in a 16-hepatocyte model of
hepatic zonation whose enzyme gradients are taken from measured human spatial
proteomics, and ask how treatment timing, ingestion pattern, and the
zonation representation interact. NAC is modelled as a GSH-synthesis boost
driven by the plasma profile of the standard 21-h IV protocol (elimination
half-life 2 h), with the peak boost calibrated to the classic recovery
criterion that NAC restores detoxification capacity rather than preventing
initial glutathione depletion. The model reproduces the clinical 8-hour rule:
protection collapses between 4 and 12 h at both the simulated 4 g and 16 g
single doses. Under measured gradients — which are shallower and, for GSH
synthesis, unzonated — the window widens substantially (protection at 8 h:
7% vs 1% at 4 g; 5% vs 1% at 16 g), because a uniform synthesis gradient
maintains a larger pericentral detoxification reserve. At fixed total dose,
staggered ingestion changes peak adduct burden by only −2 to −4%: the danger
of staggered overdose reflects late presentation, not the pattern itself.
Conclusions are robust to saturable kinetics and absorption rates. The
zonation assumption a modeller makes propagates from injury prediction into
treatment prediction — and the measured picture is the more optimistic one.
